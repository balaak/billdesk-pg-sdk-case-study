Problem
Every platform had quietly become a different product.

A legacy API under years of patches. No native Android, iOS or Flutter SDK at all. mWeb broken. Back buttons in the wrong places. Error screens that sometimes didn't name the error. And contrast failures caused by the brand colour itself.

The ask
I pitched a redesign. I was handed a platform.

I built the concept: customisation, language selection, order summary, card scanning, foreign-card handling. Then I took it to our CEO, and after that to a BillDesk co-founder. He came back wanting all of it, plus native Android and iOS.

The metric I was judged on
Merchant time-to-integrate.

Days from a merchant signing to their first live transaction. It decides whether a payment gateway grows, and the old SDK was quietly bleeding it.

Diagnosis
One product was serving three unrelated people.

My design team was the manual glue holding it together. Every brand request arrived as a one-off. That isn't a backlog problem. It's an architecture problem wearing a backlog costume.

What shipped
Three surfaces, one design language.

Every component had to work in the checkout, in Spectrum's admin views and in Playground's simulator with no special case. BillDesk UI already existed. I reused it and built the components it was missing, PulseBar and the title bar among them.

The decision I'd defend
I designed the failure path first.

Most checkout design stops at the success state. I argued for building recovery as a first-class flow: when a payment fails, the SDK retries and falls back rather than dead-ending the payer and handing them back to the merchant.

A payment that fails once isn't a lost customer. It's a customer standing still with their wallet already out.

Dynamic Currency Conversion
Pay in your own currency, not the merchant's.

Imagine you're paying abroad with a card from home. Right before you confirm, the screen offers a choice: pay in your own currency and see the exact number now, or pay in the merchant's and let your bank convert it later. That's the choice Dynamic Currency Conversion, DCC, gives the cardholder.

It looks like a small screen, but it's an easy one to get wrong: the rate on that conversion is set by the payment network, not the cardholder's own bank, so it isn't always the better deal. That's the edge case I designed around, making sure the screen never nudged the choice either way:

  • No pre-selection, no default, no steering
  • Currency versus currency, never accept/decline
  • Every disclosure at the same size, colour and font
  • Markup immediately below the rate, above the total
  • Decline once and you may not re-prompt
The proof
Everything added afterwards, without a rebuild.

Launching was never the test. The test was what the architecture could absorb over the next two years.

Then

QR

Dynamic, per-order. QR is the cross-device answer; UPI Intent is the same-device one. Choosing between them by surface is the whole design decision.

Then

CRIS

Rail ticketing at national scale. This was the integration that put the SDK at 30,000+ transactions a minute.

Then

Banking Connect

NPCI Bharat BillPay's interoperable netbanking switch, press-named "Netbanking 2.0." The payer lands in their already-authenticated bank app instead of typing a user ID and password.

Then

E-mandates

Recurring debits authorised once. The edge cases are the work: a debit crossing RBI's ₹15,000 authentication threshold turns a silent auto-debit into an interactive one, so the flow has to degrade gracefully.

Full circle
The audit opened on a contrast failure. The regulator closed it.

The brand colour itself was failing contrast, which is why Spectrum was never a free-form theming tool. Merchants got their brand; they did not get to recreate that failure.

Then RBI required payment system participants to make their systems accessible, against a Ministry of Finance banking standard that mandates IS 17802 and WCAG 2.1. One explicit rule: no placeholder text as a form label. The card entry fields were rebuilt.

Why Dubai
I've already built for the case where the card and the currency don't match.

India is UPI-dominant, with one rail carrying roughly 85% of digital transaction volume. The UAE is card-first, with Aani and Jaywan still arriving and CBUAE open finance phasing in. Different problem.

But DCC is mainstream in the Gulf. In a market where most residents are expatriates and a large share of cards are foreign-issued, the currency-mismatch case isn't an edge case. It's the default.
